# Is AI Training Data Fair Use? — Key Arguments from Authoritative Sources

| Title | Link | Summary |
|---|---|---|
| Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, Part 3: Generative AI Training | [copyright.gov](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-3-Generative-AI-Training-Report-Pre-Publication-Version.pdf) | The U.S. Copyright Office's official 2025 report concludes there is no blanket answer: training can involve prima facie infringement, and whether it's fair use depends on the specific facts. It rejects the claim that AI training is inherently "non-expressive" or like human learning, noting AI can reproduce works at a scale and fidelity humans cannot. Transformativeness exists on a spectrum — training for broad, general capability leans more transformative, while training a model to generate content that competes with the ingested works is "at best modestly transformative." The report gives the market-harm factor significant weight, citing lost sales, market dilution, and foregone licensing revenue, and treats use of pirated source material as weighing heavily against fair use. It encourages voluntary licensing markets but declines to endorse a compulsory licensing scheme. |
| Fair Use and AI Training: Two Recent Decisions Highlight the Complexity of This Issue | [skadden.com](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2025/07/fair-use-and-ai-training) | Skadden's analysis of the June 2025 rulings in *Bartz v. Anthropic* and *Kadrey v. Meta* shows both federal judges finding that training LLMs on copyrighted books was "highly transformative," supporting fair use. However, the judges diverged sharply on market harm: Judge Chhabria (Meta) was open to a "market dilution" theory where mass AI output could devalue human authorship, while Judge Alsup (Anthropic) rejected this reasoning as speculative. The article stresses both rulings were narrowly fact-specific wins driven largely by the plaintiffs' failure to show concrete evidence that the models reproduced their works or caused measurable market damage — meaning a stronger evidentiary record in future cases could flip the outcome. |
| Third Circuit Rejects Fair Use for ROSS's Copying of Westlaw Headnotes | [haynesboone.com](https://www.haynesboone.com/news/alerts/third-circuit-rejects-fair-use-for-rosss-copying-of-westlaw-headnotes) | Covering the first federal appellate ruling on AI training and fair use (*Thomson Reuters v. ROSS Intelligence*, Sept. 2026), this alert explains why the Third Circuit sided against fair use: ROSS's use of Westlaw headnotes was "highly commercial and minimally transformative" because its legal-research tool served the same basic purpose as Westlaw. The court rejected the argument that the underlying judicial opinions were freely available, holding that "ease is not a justification for copying," and recognized AI-training-data licensing as an emerging market that ROSS had unfairly bypassed. The piece notes the ruling's reach is limited, since the panel explicitly distinguished ROSS's retrieval-based tool from generative AI systems like those in the Anthropic and Meta cases. |
| Copyright and AI Collide: Three Key Decisions on AI Training and Copyrighted Content from 2025 | [ipwatchdog.com](https://ipwatchdog.com/2025/12/23/copyright-ai-collide-three-key-decisions-ai-training-copyrighted-content-2025/) | IPWatchdog synthesizes the Anthropic, Meta, and Thomson Reuters/ROSS rulings to show fair-use doctrine pulling in different directions depending on the facts. Training found to be "transformative — spectacularly so" supported fair use in Anthropic and Meta, but Thomson Reuters succeeded because ROSS's AI tool directly competed with Westlaw's core commercial product. The article frames the decisive variable as market substitution: whether the AI's output functionally replaces the market for the original works. It closes by advising that "clean, well-documented sourcing" and careful attention to where training data came from remain essential to managing legal exposure, regardless of which way future cases trend. |
| Is it legal to train AI models on copyrighted books? It's complicated | [techcrunch.com](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/23/is-it-legal-to-train-ai-models-on-copyrighted-books-its-complicated/) | TechCrunch lays out the debate in plain terms for a general audience. Proponents of fair use, echoing Judge Alsup, liken LLM training to "a writer's study of literature" — arguing copyright law targets copying and distributing expression, not the act of learning from it. Critics counter that when a company trains a model to build a product that competes directly with the works it was trained on (as in the Thomson Reuters case), the use is not transformative and should not qualify as fair use. Authors and other critics further argue that AI-generated content threatens their livelihoods by substituting for their work without consent or payment, though courts so far have not been persuaded that this harm is proven rather than speculative. |

**Takeaway:** U.S. courts have not settled on a single rule. 2025–2026 rulings suggest that training on copyrighted works is more likely to be fair use when it builds general-purpose capability and plaintiffs can't show concrete market harm (*Anthropic*, *Meta*), but less likely to be fair use when the resulting product directly competes with the market for the original works or when training data was pirated (*Thomson Reuters v. ROSS*). The U.S. Copyright Office's official position treats this as a case-by-case, fact-dependent question rather than a categorical answer.
